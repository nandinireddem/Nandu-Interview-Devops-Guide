💗 Weakness

When I work on something, I sometimes concentrate too much on very small details because I want the final result to be exactly right. The problem is that not every small detail has the same importance, and sometimes focusing on those unnecessary details can consume more time than the value they actually provide. I realised that being a perfectionist is not always a strength if it affects productivity. I still want to maintain quality, but I'm learning that being effective doesn't mean making every single thing perfect. It means knowing what actually deserves perfection.

🧩 Behavioral Questions

⭐⭐⭐⭐ Tell me about a technical problem you faced.
While working on my EKS and Terraform project, I faced infrastructure and cluster-related configuration issues. One thing I learned from those situations was that the error message is evidence, but it isn't always the complete root cause. Initially, it was tempting to change multiple configurations at once. But I realised that doing that makes troubleshooting harder because I can't clearly identify which change actually fixed the problem. So I started following a more structured approach. I identify the layer where the problem occurred, check the relevant configuration and dependencies, make one meaningful change, observe the result and then continue. That experience improved not just my technical troubleshooting, but also the way I approach problems in general.
⭐⭐⭐⭐ Tell me about a failure.
One lesson I learned from my projects is that trying to solve everything at once can actually make a problem more difficult. When infrastructure or deployment issues occurred, my initial instinct was sometimes to change multiple things quickly. But I realised that this makes it difficult to understand the actual cause. I learned to slow down, isolate the problem, change one relevant thing at a time and observe the result. That experience taught me that troubleshooting is not about making more changes quickly. It is about making the right change based on evidence.
⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ How do you handle a question you don't know?
I don't like guessing and presenting something uncertain as a fact. If I haven't worked with something directly, I would honestly say: “I haven't worked with that directly yet, but based on what I know about the underlying concept, this is how I would approach it.” Then I would explain whatever part I understand and describe how I would investigate the missing part. I believe being honest about my knowledge boundary is much better than giving a confident but incorrect answer.
⭐⭐⭐⭐ What if the interviewer challenges your answer?
I would first listen carefully. Sometimes an interviewer may challenge an answer because they want to understand whether I actually know the concept deeply. If I realise that my answer is incorrect, I would acknowledge it instead of trying to defend a wrong answer. I see technical discussions as opportunities to improve my understanding rather than arguments that I need to win.

🏆 Achievement & Project Questions

⭐⭐⭐⭐ What is your biggest achievement?
One of my biggest achievements is the transformation in the way I understand technology. Initially, I was learning tools individually. Docker was one topic. Kubernetes was another. Terraform was another. AWS was another. But when I started building end-to-end projects, especially the EKS-based DevOps project, I began understanding how these technologies connect. That changed my mindset from: “I know this tool.” to: “I understand where this tool fits and why it is needed.” For me, that shift in understanding is one of the most valuable achievements of my learning journey.
⭐⭐⭐⭐ What was the most difficult part of your project?
The hardest part wasn't learning an individual command. The challenging part was connecting multiple systems correctly. Terraform had to provision infrastructure. EKS had to provide Kubernetes. Docker had to package the application. GitHub Actions had to automate CI. Argo CD had to manage GitOps delivery. Prometheus and Grafana had to provide visibility. A problem in one layer could affect another layer. That taught me to stop looking at DevOps as a collection of tools and start looking at it as one connected system.
⭐⭐⭐⭐ What did you learn from your project?
I learned three major things. First, automation is useful only when I understand the process underneath it. Second, troubleshooting is one of the most important DevOps skills. Third, architecture matters more than the number of tools I know. I could say I know 20 or 30 technologies, but the more meaningful question is whether I understand why they exist and how they interact. That became one of the biggest lessons from my projects.
⭐⭐⭐⭐ If you had more time, what would you improve in your project?
I would improve the project in several production-oriented areas. I would add stronger automated testing, better secret management, more comprehensive security scanning, improved observability and alerting. I would also work on reusable Terraform modules, environment separation, stronger networking controls, cost optimisation and disaster recovery considerations. The existing project gave me the foundation, but these improvements would make it closer to a mature production architecture.
